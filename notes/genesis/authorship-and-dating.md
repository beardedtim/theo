notes/pentateuch/authorship.md covers the Moses/JEDP debate at the
whole-Torah level. This note is what's specific to Genesis: its internal
structure, what it's borrowing from the ancient Near East, whether the
events in it happened, and how unstable the text itself stayed.

## Shape of the book

The book is stitched together with the _toledot_ formula -- "these are
the generations of..." -- at 2:4, 5:1, 6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 11:27, 25:12,
25:19, 36:1 (repeated at 36:9), and 37:2. That's the Priestly writer's
organizing device: it narrows the lens step by step from the whole cosmos
down to Adam's line, Noah, Abraham's family, and finally the one chosen
lineage. Genesis 1 -- formal, ordered, "let there be," humanity made in
God's image -- is P. The garden story starting at 2:4b is not: the God
who shapes a man from dirt and walks in the garden in the cool of the day
is a different theological voice, older and more anthropomorphic. The two
creation accounts sitting back to back is the cleanest example in the
whole book of Genesis being a composite text rather than a single
composition.

The traditional split is between primeval history (1-11: creation,
garden, Cain and Abel, flood, Babel -- universal and mythic) and the
ancestral narratives (12-50: Abraham, Jacob, Joseph -- a family saga).
The Joseph story (37-50) reads differently again: a sustained, plotted
novella rather than a chain of short episodes, which is why it's usually
treated as its own compositional unit layered into the book (Genesis 38
and 49 look like separate insertions into it).

## Where the material comes from

The flood narrative is the strongest, least-disputed case of Genesis
drawing directly on Mesopotamian literature. George Smith announced the
Babylonian flood tablet (Gilgamesh XI) to the Society of Biblical
Archaeology in December 1872, and the parallels are close enough that
Assyriologist W. G. Lambert -- who was otherwise cautious about claiming
Genesis depended on Babylonian sources -- wrote that the birds episode in
Genesis 8:6-12 "is so close to the parallel passage in the XIth tablet of
the Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic that no doubt exists" of dependence (in
Hess & Tsumura, eds., _I Studied Inscriptions from Before the Flood_,
1994, p. 101, reprinting his 1965 argument). Gordon Wenham counts around
seventeen points of contact, in matching sequence, between the two
accounts. The Sumerian King List's implausibly long antediluvian reigns
sit behind the long lifespans of Genesis 5 the same way. These
Mesopotamian sources are all 2nd-millennium-BCE material, which is the
main reason to think the flood tradition entered Israelite culture early,
long before the book of Genesis was written down -- a written form and an
oral tradition can have very different ages.

Genesis 1's engagement with Enuma Elish is looser and more contested --
_tehom_ ("the deep") is linguistically related to Tiamat, but Genesis 1
reads as a point-by-point rebuttal (one sovereign creator, no combat,
creation by unopposed speech) as much as a borrowing, which fits a
setting where Israelite scribes were consciously answering Babylonian
cosmology, i.e. the exile.

## Whether it happened

Mainstream scholarship doesn't treat Genesis 1-11 as history at all --
it's read as theology in the idiom of ancient cosmology, using and
correcting Mesopotamian material to make claims about one God, ordered
creation, and human culpability.

Genesis 12-50 is a harder case, but the mid-20th-century consensus that
the patriarchs were real 2nd-millennium figures (Albright, Speiser,
Bright, leaning on Nuzi and Mari legal texts for "patriarchal" customs)
collapsed after Thomas Thompson (1974) and John Van Seters (1975) showed
the Nuzi/Mari parallels were cherry-picked and just as at home in the
1st millennium. What's left standing against an early date is a set of
anachronisms baked into the narratives themselves: domesticated camels as
pack animals (Gen 12, 24, 37) -- radiocarbon work by Sapir-Hen and
Ben-Yosef (Tel Aviv University, _Tel Aviv_ 40, 2013) narrows the arrival
of domesticated camels in the southern Levant to roughly 930-900 BCE, a
good three to six centuries after any plausible patriarchal date;
Philistines (Gen 21, 26), who only settle the coast around the 12th
century BCE; and "Ur of the Chaldeans" (11:28, 31), where "Chaldean" is a
1st-millennium ethnic label. None of that proves the ancestral stories
are pure invention -- a centrist position (Mazar, McCarter) holds they
carry real, old memory reshaped by long transmission -- but it does mean
the narratives as written can't be read as straight 2nd-millennium
reportage. Confessional scholars (Kitchen, Hoffmeier) push back on parts
of this (e.g. arguing camels were marginally known earlier than 1100
BCE), and that pushback is worth reading, but it's the minority position.

The Joseph novella's date is argued separately from the rest of the
ancestral material. Arndt Meinhold (1975) grouped it with Esther and
Daniel as a "diaspora novella," which points to an exilic/Persian-period
setting for at least its final form. Blum and Weingart (2017) argue
instead for a North-Israelite origin in the 8th century BCE, under
Jeroboam II -- a live disagreement, not a settled point, and one more
sign that "the ancestral narratives" aren't one thing datable all at
once.

## The text kept moving

Genesis wasn't stable even after it was "finished." The Masoretic Text,
Samaritan Pentateuch, and Septuagint diverge sharply in the primeval
genealogies (ch. 5, 11) -- the MT places the flood at Anno Mundi 1656,
the Samaritan Pentateuch around AM 1307, the LXX around AM 2242-2262
depending on manuscript. (These specific tallies, and the ~1,386-year gap
between the LXX's and MT's creation-to-Abraham totals, are most
accessibly laid out in Henry B. Smith Jr.'s "MT, SP, or LXX?", _Bible and
Spade_ 31.1, 2018 -- a confessional source, and its exact framing is
contested even among other confessional writers, but the underlying fact
that the three textual traditions disagree by centuries is not in
dispute. Mainstream scholarship doesn't treat any of these genealogies as
chronometric data to begin with.) Qumran preserves Genesis in multiple
copies (1QGen, 2QGen, 4QGen^b/d/g/k, the paleo-Hebrew 4QpaleoGen-Exod^l),
some of them siding with the LXX's longer numbers -- proof that variant
chronologies circulated in Hebrew, not just in translation.

The Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen, Aramaic, one of the original seven Cave
1 scrolls) shows Genesis already being expanded and rewritten by the 2nd
century BCE, focused on Lamech, Noah, and Abraham. 1 Enoch's Book of the
Watchers (oldest stratum, ~3rd century BCE) takes the four verses about
the "sons of God" and Nephilim in Genesis 6:1-4 and builds an entire myth
of fallen angels out of them -- a reading later echoed in Jude and 2
Peter, and later suppressed by rabbinic Judaism in favor of reading
"sons of God" as human nobility. Jubilees (~160-150 BCE) retells the
whole book under a 364-day solar calendar and its own jubilee
chronology. None of this is copying a fixed text; it's evidence Genesis
was live, authoritative, and still being argued with centuries after its
supposed final form.

The one external check on when the Torah as a whole stopped moving: the
Samaritans accepted only the five books (not the Prophets) as scripture,
which means the Torah was already a closed, authoritative unit before the
Samaritan-Jewish schism -- their temple on Gerizim went up in the mid-5th
century BCE and was destroyed by John Hyrcanus (traditionally dated 128
BCE, though some recent archaeological work argues for closer to
111-110 BCE). Either date sits comfortably after the Persian-period
window mainstream scholarship already assigns to Genesis's final
compilation.

## Takeaway

Genesis is easiest to think about as two different kinds of claim
stacked on top of each other. The literary/compositional claim -- P and
non-P material, a toledot spine, a flood story lifted from Mesopotamia,
a Joseph novella bolted on -- is about as solid as source criticism gets
anywhere in the Bible, because you can point at Genesis 1 and 2-3 side by
side and see two different Gods behaving two different ways. The
historical claim is weaker the further back it reaches: primeval history
isn't trying to be history at all, and the ancestral narratives carry
real anachronisms that I don't think honest reading can wave away, even
though I'm sympathetic to the idea that old family memory sits somewhere
underneath Abraham, Jacob, and Joseph. What I'm not persuaded by is
either extreme -- Mosaic authorship of a finished book, or a Hellenistic
invention with no older material in it at all. The flood story alone is
enough to show real 2nd-millennium tradition got into this book somehow,
long before anyone wrote "Genesis" down.
