## Tradition

Moses wrote the five books. That's the position of rabbinic Judaism, the
church fathers, and essentially every Christian tradition up to the
Enlightenment. The New Testament assumes it: Jesus refers to "the book of
Moses" (Mark 12:26), Paul argues from what "Moses writes" (Rom 10:5).

The text itself claims less. Exodus 24:4 says Moses wrote down the words of
the LORD, meaning the covenant material he had just been given. Deuteronomy
31:9 says he wrote "this law," most likely the law code in Deuteronomy.
Numbers 33:2 credits him with the travel itinerary. Nothing anywhere says he
wrote Genesis. And Deuteronomy 34 reports his death, his burial, and the fact
that nobody knows where the grave is.

Traditional readers noticed this. Ibn Ezra pointed at a handful of passages in
the 12th century that look written after Moses died and left the implication
for the reader to work out. Hobbes and Spinoza spelled it out in the 1600s.
All of that runs two centuries ahead of critical scholarship.

## Academic

Astruc started sorting Genesis by divine name in 1753. Wellhausen turned it
into a system in the 1870s: four sources, J E D P, spanning roughly the 10th
to the 5th century BCE. By 1950 this was close to universal.

It fell apart in the 70s, and much harder in Europe than in America.
Rendtorff's 1977 book is the usual dividing line. E turned out to be the weak
link, since nobody could isolate it as a narrative that stands on its own, and
divine names turned out to be a poor sorting tool.

Three positions now:

**Neo-documentarians** (Schwartz, Baden, Stackert; mostly American and
Israeli). Still four sources, but argued from narrative continuity and flat
contradictions in plot rather than from divine names or style. They treat the
redactor as a copyist splicing documents together, not an author. They also
separate identifying a source from dating it.

**Europeans** (Rendtorff, Blum, Kratz, Schmid, Römer, Otto). No J, no E.
Independent blocks of tradition (primeval history, patriarchs, exodus,
wilderness, law) joined late, with P supplying the connective thread in the
Persian period. Römer reads the finished Torah as a compromise document
between priestly, Levitical, and lay factions, and probably between Judah and
Samaria. That's his explanation for why it keeps contradictory material
instead of smoothing it out.

**Conservatives** (Cassuto, Kitchen, Hoffmeier, Sailhamer). A substantial
Mosaic core with later editorial updating. They attack the criteria as
circular, and argue that repetition and doublets are normal ancient Near
Eastern literary technique rather than evidence of splicing.

The 2016 volume _The Formation of the Pentateuch_ puts all three in one room.
It documents the deadlock instead of breaking it.

One more wrinkle: nobody can say cleanly when the book was finished. The
Qumran scrolls show the text still growing in the last centuries BCE. The
pre-Samaritan manuscripts and 4Q Reworked Pentateuch expand and harmonize
freely, and the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Hebrew behind the Septuagint are
different editions rather than different copies. Composition and copying blur
into each other.

## Takeaway

I think it would take a _very_ certain understading of the verses in order
to proclaim that the _Bible itself_ claims that Moses wrote the literal words
of the Pentateuch. Such an understanding that I can't agree the conclusion but
I am often wrong about the Bible.

It seems very clear that the Pentateuch that we read today has been _edited_ in
some way between Moses's lips and our eyes. It also clear that it was
_compiled_ by someone using other written or oral traditions. This _compiling_ or
_reworking_ of other material seems common to the time period that the _codex we read_
today would have been compiled.
